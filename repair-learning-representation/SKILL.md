# Repair Learning Representation

A representation is fit only when evidence can produce durable changes in later prediction or behavior. More notes are not necessarily more learning. Treat memory, schemas, plans, tests, skills, and other knowledge artifacts as mechanisms whose performance must be observed in use.

## Locate the representation failure

Trace one or more concrete failed transfers from evidence to later action:

> evidence observed -> interpretation formed -> lesson stored -> lesson retrieved -> prediction or decision changed

Find the broken edge. Common failures include missing provenance, facts without causal consequence, lessons stored where later work will not retrieve them, vague or oversized entries, incompatible representations, hidden contradictions, stale claims without scope, and interpretations overwriting raw evidence.

Distinguish a representation failure from lack of evidence, a bad causal model, an evaluator problem, or simple artifact failure. Preserve raw observations separately when their interpretation may change.

## Define what improvement must do

Name a behavioral deficiency in the incumbent representation and predict what a better one will change in a real future task. Useful tests concern behavior, such as retrieving the relevant constraint, noticing a contradiction, making a sharper prediction, or choosing a different action—not merely producing tidier prose.

Keep a stable anchor: representative past evidence, decisions, or held-out scenarios whose meaning should not change merely because the format changes.

## Challenge the incumbent

Change the smallest mechanism that explains the failed transfer. The solution may alter what is preserved, how it is structured, where it lives, when it is surfaced, how contradiction is represented, or something else entirely. Choose the mechanism from the observed failure rather than from a preferred knowledge architecture. Do not redesign the entire knowledge system when one broken transfer explains the failure.

Do not introduce a ledger, schema, database, graph, retrieval layer, or other persistent structure merely because such structures are conventional. Let the failed transfer determine the representation.

Compare incumbent and challenger on representative decisions, including at least one case not used to design the challenger when practical. Check whether the challenger:

- makes the prior lesson available at the moment it matters;
- changes prediction or action in the direction warranted by evidence;
- exposes uncertainty and contradiction rather than smoothing them away;
- avoids flooding future work with irrelevant context;
- preserves the ability to reinterpret original evidence.

Adopt only the parts that improve actual transfer. Migrate durable lessons carefully, retain provenance, and retire obsolete interpretations without rewriting history. Remove experimental scaffolding that has no continuing value.

Stop when the observed transfer failure no longer reproduces, representative future decisions improve, and the representation remains small enough to guide rather than bury action.
